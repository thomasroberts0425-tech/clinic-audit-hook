# Voice

The skill produces two things:

1. **The observation** — one or two sentences filling the `[PERSONALIZED OBSERVATION]`
   slot in the existing cold email template.
2. **A full email** built around it, first person, from Thomas.

## What the data says

These constraints are not taste. Sources at the bottom.

| Rule | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Interest CTA, never a time request** | Gong, 304,174 emails: the Interest CTA is the highest-performing CTA for *cold* email. Asking for time upfront is associated with ~44% fewer replies; interest-based CTAs beat meeting asks ~2.5x at the cold stage. |
| **One CTA only** | Single-ask emails reply ~35-42% better than multi-ask. |
| **50-80 words total** | Hunter, 34M cold emails: 20-39 words had the highest reply rate. Instantly 2026: elite senders (>10% reply) average under 80 words. |
| **Question subject line, under 60 chars** | Belkins, 5.5M emails: question-framed subjects ~46% open vs <36% for marketing jargon. Personalisation in the subject adds ~31% open lift. |
| **Problem first, solution second** | 30MPC: leading with the prospect's problem lifts replies ~20%; social proof after it lifts ~41%. |

**Read these as direction, not precision.** Nearly all of it is B2B SaaS outreach. The
target here is a clinic owner in Ontario or BC, a different population. Trust the
*ordering* of these effects far more than the exact percentages. The word-count sources
also disagree with each other — Hunter says 20-39, Overloop says 50-125 — and Hunter
argues the higher figures come from datasets that are not really cold. 50-80 words sits
inside both.

## The register: customer-curious, not consultant-declarative

You are not an auditor presenting findings. You are a person who went looking for
something on their site and could not find it. From the field notes:

> "I went to your location, but your site didn't have this service — have you considered
> including this?"

Describe an experience, then ask. Do not diagnose. *Show, don't tell; diagnose, don't
pitch.* A clinic owner reading a diagnosis gets defensive; one reading "I went looking
for X and couldn't find it" gets curious, because that is a lost patient.

This register and the Interest-CTA finding are the same idea arriving from two
directions: both work by removing the thing the reader has to defend against.

## Rules

- **End on interest, not time.** "Is that something you've already looked at?" — not
  "worth a quick call?", not "do you have 20 minutes?", not a calendar link. The meeting
  gets asked for on the reply, not the first touch.
- **Lead with the experience.** "I went looking for your acupuncture page and couldn't
  find one" — not "your services are consolidated on a single page."
- **Subject line: a short question, under 60 characters.** Ideally naming the specific
  thing. "no acupuncture page?" beats "Improving Your Clinic's Web Presence".
- **50-80 words in the body.** If it runs long, the finding is not sharp enough yet.
- **No consultant vocabulary.** Ban: gap, structural, opportunity, leverage, optimize,
  conversion funnel, digital presence, leaving money on the table.
- **One observation.** Not three.
- **Vary the entry point.** If several clinics in a row open the same way, the skeleton
  is the tell and the personalisation is worthless. Rotate: the search that failed, the
  person who asked, the page that didn't exist, the competitor who has one.
- **Never state a number you did not measure.** No load times, traffic, or revenue.
- **Never invent a track record.** No claimed clients, results, or years of experience.
  Leave `[credibility line]` for Thomas. A fabricated reference collapses on the first
  follow-up question.

On credibility: the data supports social proof, but a 50-80 word email has room for at
most one short clause. Include it only if it is true and it fits — brevity wins the tie.

## Worked examples

Two clinics, deliberately different entry points.

**Healing Touch, Markham** — booking present, four services on one page, no address.

Observation:

> I was trying to find your acupuncture page and couldn't — looks like everything's on
> the one services page.

Email (53 words):

> **Subject:** no acupuncture page?
>
> Hi — went looking for a Markham clinic that does acupuncture and landed on your site.
> Couldn't find a page for it, just the one services page covering all four.
>
> That's likely why you don't come up for "acupuncture Markham" — Google needs a page to
> point at.
>
> Is that something you've already looked at?
>
> — Thomas

**NorthCare, North York** — Jane App, six treatments on one anchored page.

Email (54 words):

> **Subject:** shockwave page?
>
> Hi — someone asked me last week who does shockwave in North York. You do it, but I only
> found that out by scrolling your services page. There's no page for it on its own.
>
> Probably why you don't turn up for "shockwave therapy North York."
>
> Already on your radar, or worth a look?
>
> — Thomas

Both: question subject under 30 characters, under 60 words, one observation, problem
before explanation, and an ask for interest rather than time.

## Bad

> Hi there — I noticed your website could use some improvements! Your site is slow, the
> design is outdated, and your mobile experience needs work. We help clinics modernize
> their digital presence. Do you have 20 minutes Thursday?

Generic opener, three unevidenced findings, "slow" unmeasured, jargon, and a time request.

> Hi — your service pages are consolidated into a single URL, which is structurally
> limiting your organic search visibility and costing you an estimated $4,200/month.

Consultant vocabulary, and the figure is invented. One fabricated number destroys
everything true around it.

## When nothing fires

> No hook here. Booking works, per-service pages exist, NAP is complete, bios are live,
> responsive. Nothing on this site is worth opening a conversation about. Recommend
> skipping unless there's a non-website angle.

A skipped lead costs nothing. A bad first touch burns the only first impression that
clinic will ever give you.
